Grammar notes and declension tables

Verbs that take the dative — special verbs

These verbs take a dative as their primary object, even though English translates them with a direct object:

persuadeo + dat.persuade — tibi persuadeo
resisto + dat.resist — hostibus resisto
credo + dat.believe/trust
noceo + dat.harm
placeo + dat.please
pareo + dat.obey
impero + dat.command
servio + dat.serve
faveo + dat.favour
ignosco + dat.pardon/forgive

Note: iuvo (help) and iubeo (order) look similar but take the accusative.

Dative indirect object — "give/send/tell X to someone"

These verbs take a dative indirect object for the person and an accusative direct object for the thing:

do + acc. + dat.give X to someone — donum puellae do
mitto + acc. + dat.send X to someone — epistulam regi mitto
narro + acc. + dat.tell X to someone — fabulam pueris narro
dico + acc. + dat.say X to someone — verba militi dico
trado + acc. + dat.hand over X to someone — gladium duci trado
reddo + acc. + dat.give back X to someone
scribo + acc. + dat.write X to someone
respondeo + dat.reply to someone
ad + accusative — motion toward a place

Use ad + accusative (not dative) for motion toward a place or thing:

ad urbem eoI go to the city
ad portam currithe runs to the gate
ad insulam navigathe sails to the island
ad castra contenduntthey march to the camp

Key distinction: "give the sword to the soldier" → dative (militi). "Go to the city" → ad + acc. (ad urbem). The test: is "to" expressing receiving (dative) or destination (ad + acc.)?

-io verbs (3rd conjugation)

These 3rd conjugation verbs end in -io and follow a slightly different pattern — their stem vowel is i throughout the present tense:

fugioflee

3rd declension -ium words

These nouns have -ium in the genitive plural instead of the usual -um. The ablative singular is the normal -e.

urbs, urbis f.city
mors, mortis f.death
nox, noctis f.night
parens, parentis m.parent
dens, dentis m.tooth
pons, pontis m.bridge
mons, montis m.mountain
3rd declension -ium words: same nominative & genitive singular

These nouns share their nominative and genitive singular form, and take -ium in the genitive plural:

hostis, hostis m.enemy
navis, navis f.ship
classis, classis f.fleet
civis, civis m.citizen
Same nominative & genitive singular — but not -ium

These nouns also share their nominative and genitive singular, but their genitive plural ends in -um, not -ium:

canis, canis m.dog
iuvenis, iuvenis m.young man
mare, maris n. — sea

A neuter i-stem noun: ablative singular ends in -ī (not -e), and the plural uses i-stem endings throughout.

Nom./voc./acc. are always identical (neuter rule). I-stem neuters (e.g. mare) take -ia in the plural and -i in the ablative singular instead of -e.

Reflexive pronouns

The reflexive refers back to the subject. It has no nominative and is the same for all genders, singular and plural:

Pattern: gen. sg. -ius, dat. sg. -i

All three demonstratives share the same irregular gen. sg. (-ius) and dat. sg. (-i) across all genders — eius/ei, huius/huic, illius/illi. This is also found in unus (unius/uni), solus (solius/soli), totus (totius/toti), and other pronominal adjectives.
